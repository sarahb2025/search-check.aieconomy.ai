Who can do the inspection and the AESMR in practice

  • Specialist ESM/fire protection maintenance contractors commonly do the routine inspections and testing (e.g., sprinklers, hydrants, alarms, extinguishers) and then compile the AESMR for the owner to sign (or they sign as the owner’s authorised agent).   • Who is responsible: The building owner (for apartments, usually the Owners Corporation) must ensure essential safety measures (ESMs) are maintained and an Annual Essential Safety Measures Report (AESMR) is prepared each year. (vba.vic.gov.au)

Key timing (Victoria):

  • Prepare the AESMR within 28 days before the building’s “anniversary date” (the date of the first Occupancy Permit or first Maintenance Determination affecting ESMs). For buildings built before 1 July 1994 with no later OP/MD affecting ESMs, it’s within 28 days before 13 June each year. (vba.vic.gov.au)

In Victoria, the rules come from the Building Regulations 2018 (Part 12, Division 3) and the Building Act 1993. They say the owner is responsible for essential safety measures (ESM) maintenance and the Annual Essential Safety Measures Report (AESMR). The law doesn't tie the work to one licence, so several kinds of people can do it.

Who can do it

  1. A registered building practitioner. This is usually a building surveyor, or a fire safety engineer registered with the Victorian Building Authority (VBA). Many surveying firms offer ESM audits and reports.
  1. A licensed or competent trades person for each measure. Each measure has to be maintained and checked by someone competent in that system. Examples are a fire protection contractor for sprinklers, hydrants and extinguishers, an electrician for emergency and exit lighting, and a mechanical services contractor for smoke control and air handling.
  1. A dedicated ESM or fire safety consultancy. These firms are common in Melbourne. They carry out the inspections, coordinate the trades, keep the ESM logbook and prepare the annual report.
  1. The owners corporation's building or facility manager, if they have the right competency. They can coordinate the work, but should engage competent people for the technical checks.

The annual report

  • The report must be prepared by the owner or by a person with appropriate competency.
  • It must be signed off as a statement that the ESMs have been maintained and inspected as required.
  • It must be displayed in the building, usually near the main entrance or in a visible common area.
  • It is due within 12 months of the previous one, or as the occupancy permit requires.

Choosing a provider

  • Ask about their competency. Check for VBA registration, relevant qualifications such as a fire safety or building surveying background, and professional indemnity insurance.
  • Make sure they work from your building's occupancy permit and its listed ESM schedule. Maintenance must follow what the permit requires, not a generic checklist.
  • Ask for references from other apartment buildings of similar size and age.
  • Confirm who handles the follow-up repairs and the logbook.

Penalties. Non-compliance can bring fines and municipal building surveyor orders, and it can create insurance and liability problems for the owners corporation. Your council's municipal building surveyor can also inspect and issue notices.

In Victoria, the legal duty sits with the building owner. For an apartment building, that's usually the owners corporation, acting through its strata manager. The owner can prepare the annual report themselves, but for an apartment building you'd normally hire someone competent to do it.

Who can do the inspection and report

  • Registered building practitioners. These include building surveyors, building inspectors and fire safety engineers. Many ESM consultancies are run by or employ these people.
  • Specialist ESM and fire safety consultants or auditors. These are the most common choice. Look for relevant qualifications, such as a building surveyor, fire engineering or Certificate IV/Diploma in building and fire safety background, plus experience with multi-storey residential buildings.
  • Licensed trade contractors for individual systems. Sprinklers, fire detection and alarms, emergency lighting, fire doors, hydrants and hose reels, smoke control and the like must be maintained and checked by people competent in that system. Their service records feed into the annual report.
  • Your strata or building manager. They often coordinate this, but they usually engage a consultant rather than inspect the building themselves.

What the law requires

  • The Building Regulations 2018 require the owner to maintain the essential safety measures listed on the occupancy permit, to the standard set by that permit.
  • The owner must also prepare an annual essential safety measures report each year. It must state that the measures have been assessed and found to be working properly.
  • The report must be kept with the building's records and displayed in a prominent place in the building, typically near the main entrance or fire indicator panel. It must be done by the anniversary date of the occupancy permit or certificate of final inspection.
  • The regulations require the assessor to be competent for the measures they assess. They don't name one licence that covers it, so what counts as competent can depend on the measure.

How to choose

  • Ask for evidence of relevant registration or qualifications, professional indemnity insurance and experience with comparable buildings.
  • Ask whether they'll also review the occupancy permit schedule, the logbooks and any outstanding defect notices.
  • Check whether the price includes the report itself, the physical inspection and follow-up on defects.

Because this is legally significant and the details have been amended over time, confirm the current requirements with the Victorian Building Authority (VBA) or your municipal building surveyor. If the building has combustible cladding or recent defect notices, tell the consultant up front.

AI search, explained

What is AI search?

Instead of typing keywords into Google and clicking through ten blue links, people now ask a question in ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or Perplexity and get one answer. Google itself now puts an AI answer above the results. If that answer names a business, that business gets the call. If it doesn’t name you, you’re invisible to that customer.

How does it work?

AI engines read the web, your Google Business listing, review sites and directories, then write an answer from what they find. They favour businesses that are described clearly and consistently everywhere, have pages that answer the actual question, and get mentioned by other sites. They don’t favour the biggest ad budget.

Isn’t this just SEO?

It overlaps, but it’s not the same. SEO gets you a position on a results page. AI search gives one answer, and either you’re in it or you’re not.
