How to choose a good “natural” dog food (quick checklist):

  • Complete and balanced: look for a statement that the food meets recognized nutrient profiles for your dog’s life stage (e.g., AAFCO/FEDIAF). “Natural” alone isn’t enough.
  • Named animal proteins first: e.g., “chicken,” “lamb,” “beef,” “salmon.” It’s fine if there are by‑products when they’re clearly named (e.g., “chicken liver”).
  • Minimal additives: avoid artificial colours/sweeteners; prefer natural preservatives (mixed tocopherols, rosemary extract).
  • Transparent sourcing and manufacturing: clear contact info, batch codes, and feeding guidelines; published digestibility or feeding trials are a plus.
  • Appropriate macros: puppies need higher protein/fat and DHA; seniors often benefit from higher protein with controlled calories; active dogs may need more fat.
  • Omega‑3s: look for added fish oil/linseed/flax or EPA/DHA listed.
  • Carbs and fibre: whole-food sources (oats, brown rice, sweet potato, legumes) and prebiotic fibres can aid gut health.

Pros and cons of common “natural‑leaning” formats:

  • Kibble (minimally‑additive): convenient, affordable; more processed.

  • Gently cooked/fresh: highly digestible, “whole‑food” feel; must still be complete and balanced (many aren’t unless formulated by a vet nutritionist).

Safe transition:

  • “Natural” claims: in pet food this generally means ingredients are from plant/animal/mineral sources with limited synthetic processing; added vitamins/minerals are still normal and desirable.
  • Choose the format that fits your dog and lifestyle:
  • Kibble: most budget‑friendly, easy to store. Look for named meats and moderate calorie density.
  • Wet/canned or trays: higher moisture, good for fussy eaters or those needing more hydration.
  • Air‑dried/dehydrated: minimally processed, energy-dense; feed measured amounts.
  • Gently cooked “fresh”: palatable, often short-ingredient lists; ensure it’s complete and balanced, not “topper only.”
  • Raw: only if it’s a complete & balanced recipe with strong pathogen controls; avoid for households with young kids, elderly, immunocompromised persons. Cooked alternatives are safer for most.
  • Useful nutrient targets (dry-matter basis, general guides, individual needs vary):
  • Adult maintenance: protein 22–32%, fat 10–20% (lower end if weight-prone).
  • Puppies/large-breed growth: follow large-breed–appropriate calcium (about 2.5–4.5 g per 1,000 kcal) and controlled calories; ask the company for growth data.

Transition and monitoring:

  • Transition over 7–10 days (25% new increasing to 100%). Expect small changes in stool; persistent soft stool, gas, itch, or ear issues after 2–3 weeks suggests a mismatch.
  • Reassess body condition every 2–4 weeks; adjust portions to keep ribs easily felt with a slight waist.
  • If your dog has a medical condition (e.g., kidney disease, pancreatitis, bladder stones, severe allergies), ask your vet before changing foods, therapeutic diets may be safer.

There isn't one best natural food for every dog. The right choice depends on your dog's age, size, health and budget. These are the points that matter most.

What makes a food good

  • "Complete and balanced." Look for an AAFCO statement (US) or FEDIAF statement (Europe) on the label. This matters more than whether the food is called "natural." The term has no strict legal meaning, and plenty of "natural" foods are nutritionally poor.
  • A named animal protein first. Chicken, beef, salmon or lamb is better than a vague "meat meal."
  • A reputable maker. Brands that employ a full-time veterinary nutritionist and run feeding trials are the safest bet. Purina Pro Plan, Hill's Science Diet and Royal Canin do this. Natural-leaning brands that meet the standard include Wellness, Merrick, Farmer's Dog and Ollie. Check each one's current formulas and recalls.

Common natural options

  • Fresh-cooked, human-grade (Farmer's Dog, Ollie, Nom Nom). These are highly palatable and minimally processed, and they work well for picky eaters. They are expensive.
  • High-quality kibble with whole-food ingredients. This is the most affordable and convenient choice. It is also good for dental wear and easy to store.
  • Freeze-dried or air-dried food. It is minimally processed and convenient, but it costs a lot.
  • Home-cooked food. You control every ingredient, but recipes are easy to get nutritionally wrong. Use a recipe from a board-certified veterinary nutritionist (for example, BalanceIT.com or a service like PetDiets).
  • Raw diets. I'd be cautious. They carry a risk of salmonella and other bacteria for both your dog and the people in your household, and most vets don't recommend them.

Things to watch for

  • Puppies, seniors, and dogs with allergies, kidney disease or a sensitive stomach have different needs.
  • Grain-free isn't automatically better. The FDA has investigated a possible link between some grain-free diets and heart disease (DCM) in dogs. Unless your dog has a diagnosed grain allergy, grains are fine.
  • Switch foods gradually over 7–10 days to avoid stomach upset.
  • Never feed grapes, raisins, onions, garlic, chocolate, xylitol or macadamia nuts.

AI search, explained

What is AI search?

Instead of typing keywords into Google and clicking through ten blue links, people now ask a question in ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or Perplexity and get one answer. Google itself now puts an AI answer above the results. If that answer names a business, that business gets the call. If it doesn’t name you, you’re invisible to that customer.

How does it work?

AI engines read the web, your Google Business listing, review sites and directories, then write an answer from what they find. They favour businesses that are described clearly and consistently everywhere, have pages that answer the actual question, and get mentioned by other sites. They don’t favour the biggest ad budget.

Isn’t this just SEO?

It overlaps, but it’s not the same. SEO gets you a position on a results page. AI search gives one answer, and either you’re in it or you’re not. A lot of businesses that rank fine on Google are nowhere in AI answers, because the content that wins a ranking isn’t the content an AI can quote. Already have an SEO agency? Keep them. We handle the AI search side and work alongside them. No agency? We do both, Google and AI, in one plan.

Why does the answer change each time?

AI answers aren’t fixed like a ranking. Ask twice and the names shift. That’s why this page is a snapshot and a full audit measures how often you’re mentioned across many runs and many questions, not just one.

How do you measure success?

At kick-off we agree the ten questions your customers actually ask. Then we track how often each AI engine names you on those questions, run many times over, because answers shift. That share of mentions is the score. We report it monthly against the starting point, alongside your Google rankings, so you can see movement engine by engine rather than taking our word for it.

What can I do to fix this?

Most of it is practical work on your own website and listings: a clear description of what you do and where, pages that answer the questions your customers actually ask, consistent details everywhere you appear, reviews, and the behind-the-scenes setup that lets AI engines read your site properly. Some of it you can do yourself.
